# Legal pages — research, decisions and open questions

> **Status:** drafts prepared 18 September 2026 for review by an Algerian lawyer before publication.
> Nothing here is legal advice and nothing here guarantees compliance. The Arabic documents in this
> folder are publication-quality drafts; several clauses depend on company facts that do not exist
> yet, and those are marked in the text with square brackets.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Founders Coffee today is a free meetup platform **run as a market-test beta by a natural person,
with no company formed behind it**. An entity is intended if the test succeeds — along with physical
coffee spaces for founders — but it does not exist yet, and the documents are drafted on that fact
rather than around a placeholder for it. Members create an account with an email code,
optionally fill a profile, browse cities, RSVP, and host. Hosts publish an event, see who said they
were coming, and after the event mark who actually turned up. Nobody pays anything, there is no
direct messaging, and there is no advertising or analytics tracking in the browser.

That shape decides most of the drafting:

- **The platform is not the organiser.** A member who publishes a meetup is the organiser of it.
  This is the single most important distinction in the whole package and it is stated in the Terms,
  restated in the Organizer Terms, and reflected in the Community Guidelines' enforcement section.
- **Two clauses carry unusual weight for this product.** The first is attendance: a host records
  whether a named member showed up. That is personal data about one member created by another, and
  it is not correctable through the interface. The second is that all processing happens on
  infrastructure outside Algeria.
- **Cookies are genuinely simple.** The app sets a session cookie, a language cookie and a region
  cookie. It sets nothing for analytics and nothing for advertising. The Cookie Policy says that,
  rather than filling a page with trackers the product does not run.
- **The ticketing policy is written but not in force.** It is drafted so it can be switched on, and
  it carries a warning that is the most consequential finding of this research: under Law 18-05 an
  e-commerce activity in Algeria must be registered with the CNRC and must publish **a site hosted
  in Algeria under a `.com.dz` extension**, with payment settled into an Algerian account. Paid
  events are therefore not a pricing decision; they are an entity, hosting and domain decision.

**Having no entity is a drafting input, not a drafting problem.** It settles more than it opens.
Law 18-05 and the consumer-protection regime attach to a supplier of goods or services _for
consideration_, so a free beta sits largely outside them — which is why the ticketing policy is
dormant structurally rather than by choice: an unregistered natural person cannot lawfully take
event payments under that regime at all. Law 18-07 is the opposite: it defines the controller as a
natural **or** legal person, so every data-protection obligation applies in full, personally, today.
The line that triggers incorporation is therefore the first dinar charged, not "when we grow".

The corresponding cost is that there is no liability shield. Every limitation clause in the Terms
now protects one person's own patrimony, which makes §2 of the Terms — _we are not the organiser,
not the venue operator, not a party to the arrangement_ — the most load-bearing text in the package.

One thing blocks publication outright: the ANPDP declaration and the transfer position that goes
with it. It is listed in section 4.

**Published state.** Six of the seven documents are live in the product, under `/terms`, `/privacy`,
`/cookies`, `/community`, `/organizers` and `/legal`, in Arabic for every locale — French and
English readers get a notice naming Arabic as the authoritative version, above the full Arabic text.
The ticketing policy is deliberately not routed. The published copy is generated from the Markdown
in this directory into `apps/ui/src/content/company/*.ts`; **the two must be edited together**, and
`pages.test.ts` guards the invariants that can be checked mechanically — no editorial note reaches
the public text, no unresolved bracket survives, and every cross-document link resolves to a real
company route.

Two cross-cutting observations:

1. **Every one of them separates the contract from the rules.** The Terms are the agreement; the
   Community Guidelines are the behaviour. We have followed that, because the Guidelines are the
   document people will actually read.
2. **None of them is a useful model for privacy in Algeria.** All six are drafted against GDPR or
   US state law. Their _structure_ (data categories, per-category purposes) is worth borrowing; their
   _legal bases and deadlines_ are not.

An attempt was also made to find Algerian consumer platforms publishing full Arabic legal pages as a
register reference. Nothing substantial was located — Algerian platforms in this segment tend to
publish short French pages or none. That absence is itself worth knowing: there is no local house
style to imitate, so the Arabic below aims at formal, plain Modern Standard Arabic of the kind used
in Algerian commercial and administrative writing.

## 4. Information that must be confirmed before publication

### Blocking — the pages cannot be published without these

1. ~~**The operator's full name.**~~ **Resolved.** Amine Yagoub is named as the natural person operating the beta, in the Legal Information page, Terms §1 and Privacy Policy §1, written as `أمين يعقوب (Amine Yagoub)` so the Arabic and Latin spellings are both on record. Two related calls were made and should be reviewed:
   - **No postal address is published.** The Legal Information page says it is provided on request. There is no general Algerian obligation to publish one, Art. 11 of Law 18-05 does not apply while everything is free, and publishing a private individual's home address is a real cost. This changes at incorporation, when the registered office becomes the address of record.
   - **No ANPDP declaration status is published.** Art. 32 requires telling the data subject who the controller is, the purposes, the recipients and the transfers abroad — all of which the Privacy Policy does. It does not require publishing a declaration receipt number. Saying nothing is not the same as the obligation going away; see item 3.
2. **Registered office address, commercial register number, NIF, and NIS.** _Not applicable at this stage_ and now written as such rather than left as empty brackets — an empty field reads as an oversight, "no company has been formed" reads as a fact. These become blocking again at incorporation.
3. **The ANPDP declaration.** Not yet made, as far as this research can tell. It is a legal precondition to processing (Arts. 12–14), the receipt arrives within 48 hours, and Art. 47 attaches a 500,000 DA fine to failing to notify. Art. 14 item 5 requires declaring the nature of data transferred abroad — which means the vendor list below must be settled before filing, not after.
4. **The position on transfers abroad.** Every byte of member data sits on infrastructure outside Algeria. Counsel must decide whether to seek ANPDP authorisation under Art. 44, rely on the Art. 45 derogations (express consent and contract necessity), or both. **The published policy now commits to a position**: §5 states that Art. 45 is the basis relied on today, not a prior Art. 44 authorisation, and undertakes to update the section if an authorisation is later granted. That is a drafting decision made to avoid publishing an empty placeholder, and it is the single sentence most in need of a lawyer's confirmation.
5. **The data protection delegate (DPO)** required by Art. 41 bis. A new question at this stage: whether the obligation reaches a sole natural-person controller running a beta, and who the delegate would be if it does. The Legal Information page now names a _contact point_ rather than a designated delegate, and flags the question in an editorial note, because announcing a designation that has not happened is worse than saying nothing. Needs an opinion.
6. **Minimum age.** The drafts say 19, following civil majority. If the product intends to admit students below that, a guardian-consent mechanism is required by Art. 8 and does not exist in the code.
7. **The contact address.** The product currently uses `contact@founders.coffee` for everything including privacy requests. Confirm whether a separate privacy address is wanted; the drafts assume one address and say so.

8. **Retention periods.** The drafts state periods that are defensible and that the product can actually honour. They are proposals, not observed behaviour: no retention job exists today beyond the profile-asset sweep. Each period in the Privacy Policy needs an owner and a scheduled job before it is a true statement.
9. **Account deletion.** The schema has `account_state` values `active`, `closing`, `deleted`, but no code path uses `closing` or `deleted`. Until a deletion flow exists, the Privacy Policy's erasure section is a promise fulfilled manually. That is acceptable under Art. 35 — which requires action within 10 days, not a self-service button — but it must be someone's job.
10. **Contesting an attendance mark.** A host records whether a named member attended. The member cannot see or correct that record. Art. 35 gives them the right to rectification, free, within 10 days. The Privacy Policy routes this to the contact address; a product surface would be better.
11. **What the embeddings job processes.** `apps/worker-jobs/src/jobs/embeddings.ts` sends documents to Workers AI and Vectorize. If those documents are only event titles and descriptions, the Privacy Policy is accurate as drafted. If they ever include profile text or feedback comments, the policy and the ANPDP declaration both change.
12. **Whether the public member profile is indexable.** The route `/u/$userId` is public. The visibility flags (`publish_interests`, `publish_spoken_languages`, `publish_professional_link`) all default to `false`, which is the right default and is described as such. Confirm whether search engines are allowed to index these pages.
13. **SMS.** `sms_fallback_enabled` and `sms_consent_at` exist, and Twilio is provisioned for same-day cancellation disruption. Confirm whether SMS is live in production; the Privacy Policy describes it as reserved for urgent event disruption and used only with recorded consent.
14. **One cookie is described conditionally.** The Cookie Policy says a short-lived technical cookie "may" be set by the bot-protection layer during sign-in. Cloudflare's edge sets such a cookie in some configurations and not others; nobody verified which applies here. Check the response headers on the deployed sign-in page and make the sentence definite either way.
15. **Payment model.** Nothing in the ticketing policy can be finalised — fees, payout timing, refund windows — until a provider and a business model are chosen, and until the Law 18-05 Art. 8 question below is answered. Incorporation now sits ahead of all of it as item 1 of that document's own checklist.
16. **Whether the free beta really falls outside Law 18-05.** The drafting relies on it: the e-commerce regime addresses the _fournisseur électronique_, a commercial supplier transacting for consideration, and no meetup on the platform costs anything. The inference is sound but it is an inference, and it carries the ticketing policy's entire dormancy argument. Confidence: **Needs legal verification.** If it is wrong, Art. 8's `.com.dz` hosting requirement becomes live immediately rather than at the first dinar.
17. **Beta data resets.** Terms §11 now reserves the ability to reset databases during the beta, with prior notice. If no reset is ever intended, the clause should be removed rather than kept as a convenience; if it is intended, the notice it promises needs a channel.

## 12. Final risks requiring professional review

1. **Transfers abroad are the central legal exposure.** The architecture is entirely foreign-hosted and Art. 44 makes authorisation the default route. The Art. 45 derogations are available but neither is free: express consent can be withdrawn at any time under Art. 7, and "necessary for performance of the contract" is a defensible but untested characterisation of running a meetup platform on a global edge network. This needs a decision by a lawyer, in writing, before the ANPDP declaration is filed.
2. **No hosting safe harbour was found.** Drafting has been conservative: we commit to acting on notice and we do not assert immunity. If counsel locates an Algerian basis for intermediary protection, the Terms can be strengthened. If not, the moderation commitments in the Community Guidelines are load-bearing and must be resourced.
3. **Broad liability exclusions are unlikely to hold.** The US-style "AS IS, no liability whatsoever" clause used by every platform reviewed does not translate. The drafts limit liability proportionately and expressly do not attempt to exclude liability for personal injury or for the platform's own serious fault. A lawyer should confirm the limits against the Civil Code.
4. **The attendance record is the most legally awkward feature in the product.** One member creates a factual assertion about another member's conduct, it is retained, it feeds trust signals, and the subject cannot see or correct it. Art. 35 applies to it squarely. Both a policy answer (drafted) and a product answer (not built) are needed.
5. **Paid events require an Algerian entity, an Algerian-hosted `.com.dz` site, CNRC registration and domain deposit, and settlement into an Algerian bank or Algérie Poste account** (Law 18-05 Arts. 8, 9, 27). This is not a payments integration; it is a second deployment target and possibly a second legal entity. The roadmap should be replanned around it, and the ticketing policy stays dormant until it is.
6. **The 5-day breach rule needs a scope opinion.** Our reading of the _Journal Officiel_ places Art. 45 bis 8 inside the law-enforcement title, against the grain of most published commentary. If commentary is right and we are wrong, nothing breaks — we adopt 5 days internally either way. If we are right and someone drafts to the commentary, they will misstate the law. Get the opinion.
7. **Cookie consent is unresolved in Algerian law.** We sidestep it by running no analytics or advertising cookies. The moment a product analytics tool is added, the question becomes live and a consent mechanism is needed, with no local guidance on what it must look like.
8. **Operating without an entity puts personal assets behind every clause.** This is a business risk more than a drafting one, and it is the reason the non-responsibility clauses matter so much: an incident at an independent meetup, a defamatory profile, a fraudulent fundraising claim posted by a member — each lands on a natural person with no corporate veil. Nothing in the documents can change that; only incorporation can. Worth weighing against how long the beta is expected to run.
9. **The controller will change hands.** When the entity is formed, the controller changes from a person to a company. The Terms §15 and the Privacy Policy §12 now commit to advance notice and to preserving the right to close an account before the transfer. That commitment has to be honoured in the product when the time comes — a silent update would be exactly the kind of change Art. 32 exists to prevent.
10. **The Arabic documents have not been reviewed by an Algerian lawyer.** They were drafted from the primary texts and from the product's actual behaviour, and they were edited for register. That is not the same as legal review.

## 13. Quality-control review

Three review passes were run over the Arabic drafts before they were handed over. Problems found were
fixed in the documents rather than listed as caveats; this section records what changed so a reviewer
knows where the drafts were weakest.

### Reviewer 1 — Algerian legal reviewer

- **The Art. 32 "obligation to reply and its consequences" element was missing.** The Privacy Policy
  described what is collected but never said which fields are mandatory or what follows from
  declining. A short passage was added to §2.1 stating that name and email are required to create an
  account, that without them no account can exist, and that everything else is optional with no
  consequence beyond the one feature it powers.
- **The right of access was stated more generously than the law.** Art. 34 lets a controller refuse
  manifestly abusive requests, with the burden of proof on the controller. The draft omitted both
  halves. §7 now states the limit _and_ states that the burden falls on us, which is the part a
  member needs to know.
- **The breach clause was checked against the primary text rather than the commentary.** It commits
  to Art. 43 ("without delay") and separately adopts five days as a ceiling, without attributing the
  five days to an article that, on our reading of JO 48/2025, addresses law-enforcement processing.
  The wording survives either outcome of the scope question in §3.1.

### Reviewer 2 — Product lawyer

Three statements did not match what the code does. All three were corrected against the source.

- **"Organizers write the notification text."** They do not. Notification templates are fixed and
  there is no custom-message field anywhere in the product. The Organizer Terms said otherwise,
  which would have promised a capability that does not exist and implied a bulk-message channel that
  is deliberately absent. §8 was rewritten: the platform sends standard notices, the organizer's
  channel to attendees is the event description, and using the roster to reach people off-platform
  is prospecting.
- **"The organizer sees your name in the roster."** True but incomplete, and the incompleteness was
  the interesting part. `listCloseoutRoster` selects `userId` and `name` only. The Privacy Policy now
  states positively that the organizer sees the name **and not** the email or phone, and that the
  product gives them no way to message a member directly.
- **"We do not show your feedback attributed to you."** Understated. `feedbackTally` excludes
  comments from the host projection on purpose, because on a four-person meetup three comments
  identify their authors. That reasoning is now in the policy in plain words. It is a real privacy
  decision already made in the code and it deserved to be visible.

### Reviewer 3 — Arabic legal editor

- Checked for the standard machine-translation tells and the filler the brief called out. `بشكل`,
  `يرجى`, `نحن نسعى`, `عبر الإنترنت` and `نحترم خصوصيتك` appear zero times across the seven
  documents; `من خلال` appears once.
- Kept the statutory term `المعطيات ذات الطابع الشخصي` wherever the text refers to the law, and the
  ordinary `بيانات` in explanatory prose, rather than forcing one register throughout.
- Rewrote openings that had settled into the same shape. The Community Guidelines now open on what
  the product is for rather than on a definition, and the Privacy Policy's §2 opens by refusing the
  "to improve your experience" formula explicitly instead of using it.
- Left sentence lengths deliberately uneven. Legal Arabic that is uniformly long reads as
  translation; uniformly short reads as a checklist.
